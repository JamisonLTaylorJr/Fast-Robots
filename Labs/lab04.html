Battery Discussion

We were instructed to power the Artemis and the motor drivers/motors using separate batteries. The reasons are:

  • Voltage / power limitations: the motor battery is 3.7 V, which is not ideal for powering all electronic components together (especially when motors draw current and cause voltage droop).
  • Noise isolation: motors and motor drivers introduce electrical noise (switching + current spikes). If everything shares power, measurement electronics can show extra noise and errors. Using separate batteries improves stability and measurement reliability.

Power Supply Settings Discussion

Reasonable settings for the power supply are to run it at 3.7 V, because this matches the battery voltage we were given to use for the motors.

Also, based on the datasheet for the dual H-bridge motor driver, the valid motor supply voltage range is 2.7 V to 10.8 V. This means I could choose a voltage anywhere in that range (while staying within safe limits) and the driver would still operate correctly.

Lower Limit PWM Values

I experimentally determined the minimum PWM values required for the robot to begin moving (overcoming static friction) for forward motion and on-axis turning. I also used a calibration constant to better match the two motors.

Motion Type Minimum PWM Notes
Forward (Left Motor) 60 Lowest value where the left wheel consistently started moving forward.
Forward (Right Motor) 80 Using a calibration constant of 1.23 to match the motors.
Turn Left 150 Lowest PWM where the robot could turn left without stalling.
Turn Right 160 Lowest PWM where the robot could turn right without stalling.
How I Found These Values

I started at a PWM where the wheels could spin under the robot’s own weight, and then decreased the PWM until the wheel barely moved. I then reduced it one step further to find where the motor could not overcome its initial torque (static friction) from rest.

Discussion

One difficulty with calibration was that one wheel had a larger initial torque to overcome than the other. This made it hard to drive straight because the slower motor would sometimes stop spinning or take a moment to start, while the other wheel was already moving.

My solution was to increase the PWM signal. Increasing the duty cycle helped the slower motor overcome its initial torque more consistently and run more smoothly, which improved straight-line driving.

PWM Frequency + “Keep-Running” PWM

analogWrite() PWM Frequency

The analogWrite() function generates PWM at a fixed frequency determined by the microcontroller timers. For small DC motors driven by an H-bridge, this PWM frequency is generally fast enough because the motor’s inertia averages the pulses. At lower PWM frequencies, the main downsides can be audible noise and slightly jerkier behavior at very low duty cycles.

Would Faster PWM Help?

Manually configuring timers to generate faster PWM (often > 20 kHz) could provide benefits such as:

  • Quieter motors (PWM above the audible range)
  • Smoother motion at low speeds (less torque ripple)
  • Potentially reduced low-frequency switching noise coupling into measurements

However, changing timer settings can cause conflicts with other timing-dependent functions and libraries, depending on the platform and pins used.

Start PWM vs Keep-Running PWM (Experimental Plan)

In addition to the minimum PWM needed to start moving (static friction), I also tested the minimum PWM needed to keep the robot moving once it was already in motion (kinetic friction). The procedure was:

  1. Apply a short “kick” PWM that reliably starts motion.
  2. After the robot begins moving, drop PWM to a lower value.
  3. Reduce the keep-running PWM until the robot stalls, then record the minimum stable value.
